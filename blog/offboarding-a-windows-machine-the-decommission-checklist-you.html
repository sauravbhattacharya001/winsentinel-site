Offboarding a Windows Machine: The Decommission Checklist Your Fleet Actually Needs

Retire the identity before you wipe the disk: a six-step decommission checklist that keeps a forgotten laptop from becoming a live credential.

The machine nobody remembers to retire

Every fleet has one: the laptop in the storage closet, the desktop under a desk in a closed branch office, the VM nobody logged into for nine months. It still has a domain account. It still has cached credentials. It still has a VPN certificate. And because nobody ever formally retired it, every security control that depends on the machine being known quietly stops applying to it.

Onboarding gets a checklist. Offboarding a machine almost never does. That asymmetry is how a retired laptop becomes an attacker's quietest foothold — a device with valid credentials, no patching, no monitoring, and no owner.

Why a decommissioned machine is still a live asset

A machine stops being patched the day its owner leaves, but its identity does not expire on the same schedule. Consider what a forgotten machine still holds:

  • Cached domain credentials — usable for offline attacks and pass-the-hash if the disk is recovered.
  • Machine account and Kerberos tickets — valid until the account is disabled, not until the user leaves.
  • VPN and Wi-Fi certificates — network access that outlives employment.
  • BitLocker recovery keys in escrow — useful, but only if you can prove the disk was actually wiped.
  • Local admin accounts — often with passwords set once, years ago, and shared across the fleet.

None of this is exotic. It is the ordinary residue of a machine that was set up carefully and retired casually.

The decommission checklist

Run this in order. Each step closes a door that the next step assumes is already shut.

  1. Freeze the machine first. Disable the computer account in Active Directory and revoke its certificates before you touch the hardware. This kills network access immediately, even if the wipe takes a week.
  2. Capture the evidence. Run a final audit and export the findings. If the machine is ever implicated in an incident later, this snapshot is your proof of what its posture was on its last day.
  3. Revoke, then remove. Revoke VPN and Wi-Fi certificates, remove the machine from your management and inventory tools, and delete its entries from any allowlists keyed to its hostname or MAC address.
  4. Wipe, don't just delete. A factory reset is not a wipe. Use a full-disk wipe or a BitLocker-encrypted drive reset followed by key destruction, and record the wipe certificate with the asset record.
  5. Close the asset record. Mark the asset retired in your inventory with a date, a reason, and the name of the person who signed off. An asset with no status is an asset that will be "found" again in six months.
  6. Verify from the outside. A week later, check that the machine account is still disabled, the certificates are still revoked, and no logon attempts have come from it. A retirement that nobody verifies is a hope, not a control.

Where this breaks at fleet scale

On one machine, the checklist above is an afternoon. On a hundred machines, it is a process — and processes fail in predictable ways. The two most common failures:

  • The handoff gap. HR knows the employee left; IT learns about it two weeks later; the machine sits live in between. Tie the decommission trigger to the HR offboarding event, not to someone remembering to file a ticket.
  • The partial retirement. The machine gets wiped, but the computer account stays enabled and the certificate stays valid. The hardware is clean; the identity is still armed. Step one exists precisely because step four tempts people to skip it.

The fix is boring and therefore effective: a single queue of machines pending retirement, reviewed weekly, where a machine leaves the queue only when every step is checked off. A machine that has been wiped but not revoked stays on the list. A machine that has been revoked but not wiped stays on the list. The list is the control.

The one-line version

Retire the identity before you wipe the disk, verify the retirement a week later, and keep a queue until every step is done. A machine is not retired when it is gone — it is retired when nothing it ever held can still get in.
