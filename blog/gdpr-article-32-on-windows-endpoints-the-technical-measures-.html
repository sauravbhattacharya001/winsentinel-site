GDPR Article 32 on Windows Endpoints: The Technical Measures Your Machines Already Need

GDPR Article 32 demands appropriate technical measures for personal data. Here is how Windows endpoints map to each one, and how to prove it with audit evidence.

GDPR Article 32 is short, plain, and easy to underestimate. It requires controllers and processors to put in place "appropriate technical and organisational measures" to keep personal data secure, and it names four of them: pseudonymisation and encryption, ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability and resilience, the ability to restore access after an incident, and a process for regularly testing those measures. For a Windows endpoint that holds personal data, every one of those maps to a concrete setting you can check today.

Encryption is the first line, not the last

Article 32 names encryption first for a reason: encrypted data that is lost or stolen is a much smaller incident than plaintext data. On a Windows machine that means BitLocker on the system drive, BitLocker to Go on removable media, and a healthy TPM underneath both. WinSentinel's Encryption Audit checks BitLocker status, TPM availability, and certificate store health on the machine, while the USB and Removable Media Audit checks whether BitLocker to Go actually covers the USB drives people plug in. The gap this catches most often is the laptop that is encrypted at the office and unencrypted on the spare drive in a drawer.

Confidentiality is an access problem

Encryption protects data at rest. Confidentiality day to day is about who can log in and what they can reach. The Account Audit checks local user accounts, admin membership, and password policy, and the Identity and Credential Audit looks for admin sprawl, stale accounts, and password-never-expires flags. A stale admin account on a machine that processes personal data is an Article 32 failure waiting for an auditor to find it, because the regulation asks whether access is limited to what each person needs, not whether anyone has complained yet.

Resilience means restore, not just backup

Article 32 asks whether you can restore availability and access to personal data in a timely manner after an incident. A backup that has never been restored is a hope, not a control. The Backup Security Audit checks Volume Shadow Copy, System Restore, File History, and backup recency, and it also checks ransomware resilience, because a backup that shares the same credentials as the machine it protects is not a restore plan. Test the restore on a schedule and keep the result, because the auditor will ask for the record, not the intention.

Testing is a process, not an event

The fourth measure is the one organisations skip: regularly testing, assessing, and evaluating the effectiveness of the measures. An annual penetration test is not the same thing. The Audit Policy Audit checks whether the machine's advanced audit policy actually records the security-relevant events, and Event Log Analysis looks at failed logins, lockouts, and privilege escalation in the logs. If the policy is off, the testing process has nothing to test with. The Privacy Audit covers the other side of the same coin: telemetry level, diagnostic data, location tracking, and clipboard sync, which are the settings that decide whether the machine itself is quietly exfiltrating personal data.

What the auditor actually wants

An auditor does not want a promise that your endpoints are secure. They want evidence: a list of machines, the state of each control, and a date. That is the practical shape of Article 32 compliance on Windows. Run the checks, fix what fails, keep the output, and repeat on a schedule. The regulation never asks for perfection. It asks for measures that are appropriate to the risk, and for proof that they are in place and working. A machine that can show its BitLocker status, its admin list, its audit policy, and its last restore test is a machine that can answer the question.
